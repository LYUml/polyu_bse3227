Air diffuser type 
 
Directional diffuser 
 
 
Directional diffusers include 4-way diffusers, 3-way diffusers, 
2-way diffusers and 1-way diffusers. The 2×2 ft 4-way 
diffuser is the most common type of HVAC diffuser.

Linear Slot Diffuser 
 
Linear slot diffusers include 4-slot linear diffusers, 3-slot 
linear diffusers, 2-slot linear diffusers and other numbers of 
slots depending on the manufacturer or upon request. The 
more common ones are 3-slot and 2-slot. The number of slots 
in linear diffusers corresponds to the amount of airflow. The 
higher the airflow requirement, the more slots it needs to 
have. Linear slot diffusers always have two lengths; 600mm 
and 1200mm. 
Round Diffuser 
 
 
Round diffusers are similar to directional diffusers but come 
in round shapes. They typically offer better air distribution 
than directional diffusers. They can also offer a modern and 
visually appealing look.  
 
Generally, the size of supply air diffusers should be based on air flow requirement. Physical 
dimension is the length, width and height of a supply air diffuser. Meanwhile, the neck size is 
the size of the connection between the diffuser and the supply air duct. A 4-way directional 
diffuser with physical size of 2 ft length by 2 ft width is the most common type of air diffuser. 
As for the neck size, the bigger the size, the more airflow a supply air diffuser can discharge 
without generating substantial noise. M ost supply air diffusers have a minimum neck size of 
150 mm and a maximum neck size of 350 mm in diameter.

Determination of supply air diffuser locations using ADPI  
 
For standard directional diffusers, the typical air flow rate ranges from 50 to 150 L/s . So, the 
required number of supply air diffusers can be roughly calculated from the total supply air flow 
rate. Diffusers should be distributed evenly across the space to ensure uniform air distribution.  
 
To assess how well a diffuser can distribute conditioned air throughout a space, we may check 
the Air Diffusion Performance Index (ADPI) , which is a critical metric used in AC  design to 
evaluate the effectiveness of air distribution systems . ADPI measures the percentage of the 
occupied zone that receives air within a specified temperature range of the desired setpoint. A 
higher ADPI value indicates better air distribution and comfort. An ADPI of 80% or higher is 
typically considered acceptable for office environments. ADPI provides an assessment of space 
diffusion for cooling mode only, but not for heating mode. 
 
Before we can check the ADPI value, we need to find out the characteristic room length (L) 
first. It is the distance from the diffuser to the nearest boundary wall in the principle horizontal 
direction of the airflow. Where air injected in the room does not impinge on a wall surface but 
mixes with air from neighboring diffuser, then the characteristic length L is one -half the 
distance between diffusers discharging air at opposite direction. 
 
 
 
Air diffusers should be selected so that the parameter T0.25/L is within the recommended range. 
T0.25 is the throw distance of an air jet. It is the distance from the diffuser to a point where the 
maximum velocity in the steam cross section has been reduced to a terminal velocity of 0.25 
m/s. For all diffuser, the terminal velocity of 0.25 m/s is selected, except in the case of ceiling 
slot diffusers, where the terminal velocity of 0.5 m/s is selected. ASHRAE recommends range 
of T0.25/L for different types of ceiling diffusers.  
 
The ADPI value should be checked for the design (maximum) supply air flow rate for each 
diffuser. If VAV system is being adopted, ADPI should be checked for the expected minimum 
flow rate too (e.g. minimum supply flow rate setting at 40% of maximum).

Return air grilles 
 
Because the locations and characteristics of return air grilles have much less influence on the 
air temperature and speed distributions in an air -conditioned space, there is much freedom in 
locating return air grilles in space air diffusion design, provided the face velocity and the noise 
emitted from the grilles stay within acceptable limits. A simple criterion is to limit the face 
velocity at or below 2.5 m/s. 
 
Face velocity = Airflow rate Effective area of grille 
 
Ductwork design and sizing 
 
The supply air is distributed from the air handling equipment to various supply air diffusers 
throughout the space. The conditioned air travels through the main ducts, then branch ducts 
and finally to the supply air diffusers. The duct layout should be simple, direct, and balanced. 
Unnecessary bends, branches, or splits in the air  ducts should be avoided because they  can 
cause turbulence, noise, and energy loss. Every place where you connect pieces together is a 
potential spot for air leaks, so the ductwork layout should be as direct as possible, reducing the 
number of connections and turns. 
 
• Main ducts should be as straight as possible, minimizing bends and turns. 
• Plan branch ducts to extend from the main trunk to distribute air evenly throughout the 
space. 
• Ducts shouldn’t be twisted to make tight turns. Use long radius bends rather than right 
angles as the sudden change in direction wastes a huge amount of energy. 
• Avoid running supply air ducts in unconditioned spaces to prevent unwanted heat gain. 
• No air diffusers should be installed in the main duct. 
 
The size of the air  ducts is based on the airflow requirements of the zone served by the air 
conditioning system. The size of your ducts affects how much air can flow through them and 
how fast it can travel. Properly sized ducts allow for optimal airflow. Undersized ducts create 
resistance, leading to increased energy consumption as the system works harder to push a ir 
through. Smaller ducts can also lead to higher air velocity, resulting in noise. The air velocities 
in ducts that are too large will not be effective at distributing air throughout the rooms.
